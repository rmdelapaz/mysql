Retrieval questions and answers
  1. One order has many lines. Where does the foreign key go? Answer: order_items.order_id (the many side).
  2. Predict the count. (Order 3 has one line; fk_items_order is ON DELETE CASCADE.)
    START TRANSACTION;
DELETE FROM orders WHERE id = 3;
SELECT COUNT(*) AS items FROM order_items;
ROLLBACK;
    +-------+
| items |
+-------+
|    17 |
+-------+
    Answer: 17: the order's one line went with it; ROLLBACK put both back.
  3. What happens, and why?
    DELETE FROM authors WHERE id = 6;
    ERROR 1451 (23000): Cannot delete or update a parent row: a foreign key constraint fails (`bookstore`.`books`, CONSTRAINT `fk_books_author` FOREIGN KEY (`author_id`) REFERENCES `authors` (`id`) ON DELETE RESTRICT ON UPDATE CASCADE)
    Answer: 1451: Tolkien has books and fk_books_author is RESTRICT. "delete or update a PARENT row".
  4. A key is declared with no ON DELETE. What does MySQL do when a parent with children is deleted? Answer: it refuses: NO ACTION behaves exactly like RESTRICT.
  5. How many links?
    SELECT COUNT(*) AS links
FROM book_categories
WHERE book_id IN (SELECT id FROM books WHERE author_id = 6);
    +-------+
| links |
+-------+
|     4 |
+-------+
    Answer: 4: Tolkien's two books are each Fiction and Fantasy. Today the same question becomes one join instead of a nested IN.
How to read the warm-up
  • Q5 slow or wrong: following keys through two tables is exactly what a join does in one step; say "after this hour, you will write that as a join".
  • Q2 or Q3 wrong: the assessment's Part A asks for delete rules with a reason; point those learners to their Session 6 relationship map before the break.

Lesson Plan

4.3

Joins

65 min
Objectives (from the lesson page)
  • Combine matching rows from two or more tables with INNER JOIN ... ON, using table aliases
  • Keep unmatched rows with LEFT JOIN, and find rows with no match using the anti-join pattern
  • Explain what RIGHT JOIN and CROSS JOIN return, and when (rarely) to use them
  • Join a table to itself to read a hierarchy such as employees and managers
  • Combine joins with GROUP BY without the two silent traps: COUNT(*) on outer joins and row fan-out
Timing in the room
10mFrame: a join pairs rows; INNER keeps pairs, LEFT keeps every left row; one joined row stands for what?
25mLive demo: INNER, aliases, LEFT, the anti-join, a join without ON, a self-join, four tables, GROUP BY, three traps
20mGuided lab: catalog_joins_starter.sql in pairs
10mStart the lesson project: the sales report, Step 1
Mental model to say

Two short lists on the board, a guest list and the RSVPs (the lesson's journal prompt): "A JOIN pairs each guest with their RSVPs and keeps the pairs: a guest who never replied is not in the result. A LEFT JOIN keeps every guest, and where there is no RSVP it writes NULL. Same lists, same condition, one more word."

Then the question that prevents the silent bugs, pointing at the board: "what does one row of this join stand for?" "Join customers to orders and one row is an order. Add order_items and one row is an order LINE: Alice's first order now appears twice. Count after that and you count lines."

Then where conditions go: "ON says how the tables connect. WHERE filters the joined rows. On a LEFT JOIN, a condition on the right table goes in ON, or the NULL rows you wanted to keep are filtered away."

Live demo (you type, they follow)

The lesson's own examples on a fresh bookstore.sql. Ask for a row count before every join: it is the habit that catches fan-out and missing ON clauses.

  1. INNER JOIN: only the matches (3m). "An order row says customer_id 1; the name lives in customers. A join pairs each row with the rows of another table that meet a condition, usually foreign key equals primary key." Point at the ON line. 
    SELECT books.title, authors.name AS author
FROM books
INNER JOIN authors ON books.author_id = authors.id
ORDER BY books.title;
    +------------------------+---------------------+
| title                  | author              |
+------------------------+---------------------+
| 1984                   | George Orwell       |
| Animal Farm            | George Orwell       |
| Brave New World        | Aldous Huxley       |
| Dune                   | Frank Herbert       |
| Fahrenheit 451         | Ray Bradbury        |
| Lord of the Rings      | J.R.R. Tolkien      |
| Pride and Prejudice    | Jane Austen         |
| The Catcher in the Rye | J.D. Salinger       |
| The Great Gatsby       | F. Scott Fitzgerald |
| The Hobbit             | J.R.R. Tolkien      |
| To Kill a Mockingbird  | Harper Lee          |
+------------------------+---------------------+
    Eleven books, eleven rows. Asimov is absent: an inner join keeps only pairs.
  2. Aliases and WHERE (2m). "b and a are short names. ON says how the tables connect; WHERE says which results you want." 
    SELECT b.title, b.price, a.name AS author
FROM books AS b
JOIN authors AS a ON b.author_id = a.id
WHERE a.name = 'George Orwell'
ORDER BY b.title;
    +-------------+-------+---------------+
| title       | price | author        |
+-------------+-------+---------------+
| 1984        |  9.99 | George Orwell |
| Animal Farm |  8.49 | George Orwell |
+-------------+-------+---------------+
    JOIN on its own means INNER JOIN. Once a table has an alias, use only the alias (Step 11).
  3. LEFT JOIN: keep every left row (3m). "Same tables, same ON, one more word." Ask: how many rows now? Then run it. 
    SELECT a.name AS author, b.title
FROM authors AS a
LEFT JOIN books AS b ON b.author_id = a.id
ORDER BY a.id, b.title;
    +---------------------+------------------------+
| author              | title                  |
+---------------------+------------------------+
| Frank Herbert       | Dune                   |
| George Orwell       | 1984                   |
| George Orwell       | Animal Farm            |
| Harper Lee          | To Kill a Mockingbird  |
| F. Scott Fitzgerald | The Great Gatsby       |
| Jane Austen         | Pride and Prejudice    |
| J.R.R. Tolkien      | Lord of the Rings      |
| J.R.R. Tolkien      | The Hobbit             |
| Aldous Huxley       | Brave New World        |
| J.D. Salinger       | The Catcher in the Rye |
| Ray Bradbury        | Fahrenheit 451         |
| Isaac Asimov        | NULL                   |
+---------------------+------------------------+
    Twelve: the eleven pairs plus Asimov with NULL. The left table is the one before the word JOIN.
  4. The anti-join (3m). "The NULLs are a tool. Keep only the rows where the right side is missing." 
    SELECT c.first_name, c.last_name
FROM customers AS c
LEFT JOIN orders AS o ON o.customer_id = c.id
WHERE o.id IS NULL
ORDER BY c.id;
    +------------+-----------+
| first_name | last_name |
+------------+-----------+
| Carol      | Williams  |
| Hank       | Taylor    |
+------------+-----------+
    Test the right table's primary key: it can only be NULL when there was no match. NOT EXISTS (Lesson 3.4) answers the same question.
  5. Books never sold (1m). 
    SELECT b.title
FROM books AS b
LEFT JOIN order_items AS oi ON oi.book_id = b.id
WHERE oi.id IS NULL
ORDER BY b.title;
    +------------------------+
| title                  |
+------------------------+
| The Catcher in the Rye |
| The Great Gatsby       |
+------------------------+
  6. A join without ON (1m). "Forget the ON and MySQL does not complain." 
    SELECT COUNT(*) AS rows_returned
FROM books
JOIN authors;
    +---------------+
| rows_returned |
+---------------+
|           110 |
+---------------+
    11 × 10 = 110: a cross join. If a join returns far more rows than expected, check the ON first.
  7. Self-join: two roles, one table (3m). "employees.manager_id points at another employee. e plays the employee, m the manager." 
    SELECT
    CONCAT(e.first_name, ' ', e.last_name) AS employee,
    CONCAT(m.first_name, ' ', m.last_name) AS manager
FROM employees AS e
LEFT JOIN employees AS m ON e.manager_id = m.id
ORDER BY e.id;
    +---------------+--------------+
| employee      | manager      |
+---------------+--------------+
| Maria Santos  | NULL         |
| Daniel Reyes  | Maria Santos |
| Priya Nair    | Daniel Reyes |
| Tom Becker    | Priya Nair   |
| Lena Park     | Priya Nair   |
| Marcus Hill   | Daniel Reyes |
| Sofia Alvarez | Marcus Hill  |
| James Okafor  | Maria Santos |
| Nina Kowalski | James Okafor |
| Ben Carter    | Priya Nair   |
+---------------+--------------+
    Maria survives only because this is a LEFT JOIN; an inner join would drop the head of the company. To walk the whole tree: Lesson 5.1 (recursive CTE).
  8. Four tables: who bought what (2m). "Each JOIN adds one table to the row you are building. Walk the keys from orders outward." 
    SELECT o.id AS order_id, o.order_date, b.title, oi.quantity
FROM orders AS o
JOIN customers AS c    ON o.customer_id = c.id
JOIN order_items AS oi ON oi.order_id = o.id
JOIN books AS b        ON oi.book_id = b.id
WHERE c.first_name = 'Alice'
ORDER BY o.order_date, b.title;
    +----------+------------+---------------------+----------+
| order_id | order_date | title               | quantity |
+----------+------------+---------------------+----------+
|        1 | 2026-01-15 | 1984                |        2 |
|        1 | 2026-01-15 | Dune                |        1 |
|        2 | 2026-02-20 | Pride and Prejudice |        1 |
|        8 | 2026-04-05 | Lord of the Rings   |        1 |
+----------+------------+---------------------+----------+
    Order 1 appears twice: one row per order LINE. Remember that before adding things up.
  9. Joins with GROUP BY: zeros included (2m). "Books per author, Asimov included. COUNT the right table's id." 
    SELECT a.name AS author, COUNT(b.id) AS books
FROM authors AS a
LEFT JOIN books AS b ON b.author_id = a.id
GROUP BY a.id, a.name
ORDER BY books DESC, author;
    +---------------------+-------+
| author              | books |
+---------------------+-------+
| George Orwell       |     2 |
| J.R.R. Tolkien      |     2 |
| Aldous Huxley       |     1 |
| F. Scott Fitzgerald |     1 |
| Frank Herbert       |     1 |
| Harper Lee          |     1 |
| J.D. Salinger       |     1 |
| Jane Austen         |     1 |
| Ray Bradbury        |     1 |
| Isaac Asimov        |     0 |
+---------------------+-------+
  10. What each customer spent (2m). "Both joins LEFT so Carol and Hank stay; COUNT(DISTINCT o.id) counts orders, not lines; COALESCE turns their NULL sums into 0." 
    SELECT
    CONCAT(c.first_name, ' ', c.last_name)           AS customer,
    COUNT(DISTINCT o.id)                             AS orders,
    COALESCE(SUM(oi.quantity * oi.price_at_time), 0) AS spent
FROM customers AS c
LEFT JOIN orders AS o       ON o.customer_id = c.id
LEFT JOIN order_items AS oi ON oi.order_id = o.id
GROUP BY c.id, c.first_name, c.last_name
ORDER BY spent DESC, customer;
    +----------------+--------+-------+
| customer       | orders | spent |
+----------------+--------+-------+
| Alice Johnson  |      3 | 58.95 |
| Dave Brown     |      2 | 45.96 |
| Frank Miller   |      2 | 41.96 |
| Grace Wilson   |      2 | 33.97 |
| Bob Smith      |      2 | 33.47 |
| Eve Davis      |      1 | 22.98 |
| Carol Williams |      0 |  0.00 |
| Hank Taylor    |      0 |  0.00 |
+----------------+--------+-------+
  11. Trap 1: an ambiguous column. 
    SELECT id, title, name
FROM books
JOIN authors ON books.author_id = authors.id;
    ERROR 1052 (23000): Column 'id' in field list is ambiguous
    Qualify it: books.id or b.id.
  12. Trap 2: WHERE on the right table (silent) (2m). "Every customer, with their orders from May on." Predict how many customers come back. 
    SELECT c.first_name, o.order_date
FROM customers AS c
LEFT JOIN orders AS o ON o.customer_id = c.id
WHERE o.order_date >= '2026-05-01'
ORDER BY c.first_name;
    +------------+------------+
| first_name | order_date |
+------------+------------+
| Dave       | 2026-06-07 |
| Frank      | 2026-05-02 |
| Grace      | 2026-05-19 |
+------------+------------+
    Three, not eight: for customers without a May order the date is NULL and WHERE drops them. The LEFT JOIN has quietly become an inner join.
  13. The fix: the condition goes in ON (1m). 
    SELECT c.first_name, o.order_date
FROM customers AS c
LEFT JOIN orders AS o
       ON o.customer_id = c.id
      AND o.order_date >= '2026-05-01'
ORDER BY c.first_name;
    +------------+------------+
| first_name | order_date |
+------------+------------+
| Alice      | NULL       |
| Bob        | NULL       |
| Carol      | NULL       |
| Dave       | 2026-06-07 |
| Eve        | NULL       |
| Frank      | 2026-05-02 |
| Grace      | 2026-05-19 |
| Hank       | NULL       |
+------------+------------+
    Eight customers; the condition decides which orders match without removing anyone.
  14. Trap 3: COUNT(*) after a LEFT JOIN (silent). "Asimov has no books." 
    SELECT a.name, COUNT(*) AS books
FROM authors AS a
LEFT JOIN books AS b ON b.author_id = a.id
WHERE a.id IN (2, 10)
GROUP BY a.id, a.name
ORDER BY a.id;
    +---------------+-------+
| name          | books |
+---------------+-------+
| George Orwell |     2 |
| Isaac Asimov  |     1 |
+---------------+-------+
    COUNT(*) counted his padded row. Count the right table's column: COUNT(b.id).
Three mistakes to expect, and how to coach each
  • Ambiguous or alias-mismatched columns (1052, 1054). Coach: "Which table does this column come from? Write it in front, with the alias." Many developers qualify every column in a join.
  • A LEFT JOIN that silently became an inner join (a right-table condition in WHERE). Coach: "Who disappeared? Carol and Hank? Then your WHERE removed the NULL rows: move the condition into ON."
  • Counting after a fan-out. Coach with the board question: "What does one row stand for here?" Then COUNT(DISTINCT ...), or count the right table's id after a LEFT JOIN.

Checks for understanding

Ask these out loud during the demo and the lab:

  • Customers JOIN orders returns 12 rows; LEFT JOIN returns 14. Who are the two extra rows? Answer: Carol and Hank, with NULL order columns.
  • How do you find the customers who never ordered? Answer: LEFT JOIN orders, WHERE o.id IS NULL (or NOT EXISTS).
  • Why COUNT(b.id) and not COUNT(*) after a LEFT JOIN? Answer: COUNT(*) counts the NULL-padded row; COUNT(b.id) skips it.
  • Why does a self-join need aliases? Answer: the same table plays two roles; without aliases every column is ambiguous (1066).

If You Run Long or Short

Running long: what to protect and what to cut first
  • Protect: the 60 assessment minutes and the 1:25 start; 4.3 demo Steps 1 to 4 and 12 to 14.
  • Cut first (saves up to 20m): the 4.3 guided lab. Learners take catalog_joins_starter.sql home; its expected output is in the file.
  • Cut second (saves 10m): the project start: Step 1 goes home with the rest.
  • Cut third (saves 7m): demo Steps 5 to 8 (books never sold, the cross join, the self-join, four tables): show the slides only.
  • If the assessment starts late anyway: keep the full 60 minutes and shorten the review to five minutes (A1 and B8 only).
Running short: what to add
  • Before the break: debug_joins.sql with the whole room (three errors and the silent fan-out).
  • After the assessment: let learners compare their B8 with a neighbor's and explain any difference in one sentence (the most instructive task to discuss).
