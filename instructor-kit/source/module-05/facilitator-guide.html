Session 5: Aggregates and Subqueries

How to teach the fifth session of MySQL Foundations: from rows to numbers. Aggregates and GROUP BY turn the books and customers into counts, totals and averages per group (with WHERE before the piles and HAVING after), then subqueries answer two-step questions in one statement: above average, the row behind the maximum, a list for IN, the NOT IN / NULL trap, correlated lookups, NOT EXISTS and derived tables.

2 lessons 3 hrs session Lessons 3.3–3.4 Session 5 of 14 · Part 1

How to Use This Guide

  • Same rhythm for both lessons: frame (one mental model) → live demo (you type, they follow) → guided lab (a kit lab: new practice on the lesson's ideas, never the lesson project) → start the lesson's own project. The lesson exercises, quiz and journal are homework.
  • Type, do not paste. Every SQL block below was run on MySQL 9.7.2 in the flat bookstore of Module 3 (Lesson 3.1's setup script, labs/_states/flat.sql) to produce the output printed under it. Ask for a prediction before every statement that prints something; read every error number aloud.
  • The lessons are the reference: keep lesson_09.html and lesson_10.html open for the diagrams (piles, the run order, inner and outer query). Labs live in labs/module-05/: _starter.sql files are handouts, _solution.sql files are yours.
  • This is the session where SQL turns from "show me rows" into "tell me something about the rows". Two ideas carry it: the piles (WHERE before, HAVING after) and inside out (the inner query answers first). Draw both and leave them on the board all session.
  • Part 1 (Sessions 1 to 7) can run on its own as a short course; nothing here depends on Part 2. Teal blocks are hands-on, amber blocks are watch-outs.
The #1 risk of Session 5: correlated subqueries eat the second half

Lesson 3.4 is 100 minutes for a solo learner and the most abstract hour of Part 1. Learners who follow the scalar subquery and the IN list often stall at the correlated one, and a room can lose twenty minutes on why author = author is always true. Protect the scalar subquery, the NOT IN trap and the walk-through of one correlated row (Steps 2, 5 to 8): those are what Session 6 and 7 lean on (Lesson 4.1 uses correlated lookups before joins exist). ANY/ALL is deliberately not in the demo (it is in the lesson and the lab); keep the derived table to one example, and send the price review's Steps 3 to 6 home. If the room is still lost at 2:00, do the correlated step of the project (Step 2) together on the projector, inner query first, instead of the lab's TODO 2.

Retrieval questions and answers

Put the five questions on the slide; learners answer in the workbook's Warm-up box without running anything, then you run the SQL ones. The real output is below each.

  1. Who comes back, and why? (AND binds before OR.) 
    SELECT first_name, city, id
FROM customers
WHERE city = 'Chicago' OR city = 'Houston' AND id > 5
ORDER BY id;
    +------------+---------+----+
| first_name | city    | id |
+------------+---------+----+
| Bob        | Chicago |  2 |
| Grace      | Chicago |  7 |
+------------+---------+----+
    Answer: Bob and Grace. AND is evaluated first, so the condition reads city = 'Chicago' OR (city = 'Houston' AND id > 5); Dave (Houston) has id 4. Parentheses say what you mean.
  2. Why does this find nobody, although two customers have no city? 
    SELECT first_name FROM customers WHERE city = NULL;
    Empty set
    Answer: nothing is equal to NULL, not even NULL. Write WHERE city IS NULL (Eve and Hank).
  3. In which order must you write WHERE, ORDER BY and LIMIT? Answer: WHERE, then ORDER BY, then LIMIT (LIMIT always last). Today adds GROUP BY and HAVING between WHERE and ORDER BY.
  4. Which three books does this show? 
    SELECT title, price
FROM books
ORDER BY price DESC, title
LIMIT 3 OFFSET 2;
    +-----------------------+-------+
| title                 | price |
+-----------------------+-------+
| To Kill a Mockingbird | 11.99 |
| The Hobbit            | 10.99 |
| Fahrenheit 451        | 10.49 |
+-----------------------+-------+
    Answer: the 3rd to 5th most expensive: OFFSET 2 skips Lord of the Rings and Dune. The tie-breaker title makes the order repeatable.
  5. Predict: how many customers outside Chicago? 
    SELECT first_name
FROM customers
WHERE city NOT IN ('Chicago', NULL);
    Empty set
    Answer: none. Lesson 3.1 warned: a NULL inside a NOT IN list makes every row unknown. Keep this one in mind: after the break, the same trap hides inside a subquery.
How to read the warm-up
  • Q1 wrong (Dave included, or Bob only): precedence is shaky. Today's HAVING conditions combine AND and OR (the lesson project's Step 4); say "parentheses" every time you see both.
  • Q2 wrong: spend one minute on NULL now: it is the main character of both lessons today (COUNT(column), AVG skipping NULL, the NOT IN trap).
  • Q5 right from more than half the room: say "you already know the trap; after the break you will meet it where it is harder to see".

Lesson Plans

3.3

Aggregate Functions & GROUP BY

60 min
Objectives (from the lesson page)
  • Summarize a table with COUNT, SUM, AVG, MIN and MAX, and predict how each one treats NULL
  • Produce one summary row per group with GROUP BY, sorted with ORDER BY
  • Keep or drop whole groups with HAVING, and explain when to use WHERE instead
  • Read and fix the errors MySQL raises for broken aggregate queries (1140, 1055, 1111)
  • Build a small inventory report as a reusable .sql script
Timing in the room
10mFrame: the cashier's slip; rows into piles, one answer per pile; the run order
25mLive demo: COUNT three ways, SUM, AVG, MIN, MAX, GROUP BY, HAVING, errors 1140, 1055, 1111
15mGuided lab: customer_summary_starter.sql in pairs
10mStart the lesson project: the inventory report, Steps 1 and 2
Mental model to say

Start with the slip: "At the end of the day the cashier does not read every receipt. She reads the totals at the bottom. That is an aggregate: many rows in, one value out. COUNT, SUM, AVG, MIN, MAX."

Then the piles, drawn on the board: "GROUP BY sorts the rows into piles that share a value, then runs the aggregate once per pile. One pile, one result row. Every column you show must be either the pile's label (the GROUP BY column) or a summary of the pile (an aggregate). Anything else, like one title from a pile of two, is a guess, and MySQL refuses to guess."

Then the run order, pointing at the seven boxes: "You WRITE SELECT first, but the server RUNS it fifth. WHERE runs before the piles exist, so it cannot count them. HAVING runs after, so it can."

Live demo (you type, they follow)

The lesson's own examples, typed live in the flat bookstore. Learners type along. Ask for a prediction before each statement and read every error number aloud.

  1. COUNT three ways (3m). "A plain SELECT gives one result row per table row. An aggregate reads many rows and gives back ONE value." Ask first: eight customers, two without a city, New York and Chicago twice each: what are the three numbers? 
    SELECT
    COUNT(*)             AS customers,
    COUNT(city)          AS with_a_city,
    COUNT(DISTINCT city) AS cities
FROM customers;
    +-----------+-------------+--------+
| customers | with_a_city | cities |
+-----------+-------------+--------+
|         8 |           6 |      4 |
+-----------+-------------+--------+
    Write the rule on the board: every aggregate skips NULL, except COUNT(*).
  2. SUM and AVG (2m). "SUM adds, AVG divides by the number of values. Why six decimals?" Let them guess before you explain: price is DECIMAL(8,2), and MySQL keeps the average exact by adding four places. 
    SELECT
    SUM(price)           AS one_of_each,
    AVG(price)           AS avg_price,
    ROUND(AVG(price), 2) AS avg_rounded
FROM books;
    +-------------+-----------+-------------+
| one_of_each | avg_price | avg_rounded |
+-------------+-----------+-------------+
|      109.90 | 10.990000 |       10.99 |
+-------------+-----------+-------------+
    Habit for the rest of the course: ROUND(AVG(...), 2) whenever a person reads money.
  3. MIN tells the value, not the row (2m). "MIN says the cheapest price is 7.99. Which book is that?" It cannot say. Sort and take one row, as in Lesson 3.2. 
    SELECT MIN(price) AS cheapest, MAX(price) AS priciest
FROM books;
SELECT title, price
FROM books
ORDER BY price
LIMIT 1;
    +----------+----------+
| cheapest | priciest |
+----------+----------+
|     7.99 |    15.99 |
+----------+----------+
+---------------------+-------+
| title               | price |
+---------------------+-------+
| Pride and Prejudice |  7.99 |
+---------------------+-------+
    Leave this open: "what if two books shared the lowest price? LIMIT 1 would hide one." Lesson 3.4 closes the gap after the break.
  4. Aggregates over no rows (2m). "Nothing costs over $100. COUNT says 0. What does SUM say?" 
    SELECT
    COUNT(*)   AS books_over_100,
    SUM(price) AS their_total
FROM books
WHERE price > 100;
    +----------------+-------------+
| books_over_100 | their_total |
+----------------+-------------+
|              0 |        NULL |
+----------------+-------------+
    COUNT answers 0, the others NULL. A report that adds a NULL to something gets NULL: COALESCE(SUM(price), 0).
  5. GROUP BY: one answer per pile (4m). Draw ten books falling into piles by author, then COUNT on each pile. "Nine authors, so nine piles, so nine rows. I show four." 
    SELECT author, COUNT(*) AS book_count
FROM books
GROUP BY author
ORDER BY book_count DESC, author
LIMIT 4;
    +---------------------+------------+
| author              | book_count |
+---------------------+------------+
| J.R.R. Tolkien      |          2 |
| Aldous Huxley       |          1 |
| F. Scott Fitzgerald |          1 |
| Frank Herbert       |          1 |
+---------------------+------------+
    The ORDER BY is not decoration: on MySQL 8.0 and later groups come back in no promised order (the lesson's Common Mistake 5).
  6. Several aggregates per group (2m). "One pile for in stock, one for out of stock, four numbers each." 
    SELECT
    in_stock,
    COUNT(*)             AS books,
    ROUND(AVG(price), 2) AS avg_price,
    MAX(pages)           AS longest
FROM books
GROUP BY in_stock
ORDER BY in_stock DESC;
    +----------+-------+-----------+---------+
| in_stock | books | avg_price | longest |
+----------+-------+-----------+---------+
|        1 |     8 |     11.49 |    1178 |
|        0 |     2 |      8.99 |     234 |
+----------+-------+-----------+---------+
    in_stock is a BOOLEAN stored as 1 and 0. The two out-of-stock books both cost 8.99.
  7. HAVING keeps whole piles (3m). "WHERE picks rows going INTO the piles. HAVING picks piles coming OUT." Point at the run-order diagram on the board. 
    SELECT city, COUNT(*) AS customers
FROM customers
WHERE city IS NOT NULL
GROUP BY city
HAVING customers > 1
ORDER BY city;
    +----------+-----------+
| city     | customers |
+----------+-----------+
| Chicago  |         2 |
| New York |         2 |
+----------+-----------+
  8. WHERE and HAVING together (3m). Read each line's job aloud: drop out-of-stock books, pile by author, keep piles averaging over $10, most expensive first. 
    SELECT author, ROUND(AVG(price), 2) AS avg_price
FROM books
WHERE in_stock = TRUE
GROUP BY author
HAVING AVG(price) > 10
ORDER BY avg_price DESC;
    +----------------+-----------+
| author         | avg_price |
+----------------+-----------+
| Frank Herbert  |     14.99 |
| J.R.R. Tolkien |     13.49 |
| Harper Lee     |     11.99 |
| Ray Bradbury   |     10.49 |
+----------------+-----------+
    Rule of thumb on the board: a condition on one row's column goes in WHERE; a condition on a group's aggregate goes in HAVING.
  9. Error 1140 on purpose (1m). "Ten titles squashed into one row. Which title goes in it?" 
    SELECT title, COUNT(*) FROM books;
    ERROR 1140 (42000): In aggregated query without GROUP BY, expression #1 of SELECT list contains nonaggregated column 'bookstore.books.title'; this is incompatible with sql_mode=only_full_group_by
  10. Error 1055 on purpose (2m). "Tolkien's pile has two titles. Which one?" Read the message from the left: number, then 'not in GROUP BY clause'. 
    SELECT author, title, COUNT(*) AS books
FROM books
GROUP BY author;
    ERROR 1055 (42000): Expression #2 of SELECT list is not in GROUP BY clause and contains nonaggregated column 'bookstore.books.title' which is not functionally dependent on columns in GROUP BY clause; this is incompatible with sql_mode=only_full_group_by
    Fix: aggregate it (MIN(title)), group by it, or drop it. Never switch off ONLY_FULL_GROUP_BY to make it go away: MariaDB already does, and silently picks a title.
  11. Error 1111 on purpose (1m). "WHERE runs before the piles exist. There is nothing to count yet." 
    SELECT author, COUNT(*) AS books
FROM books
WHERE COUNT(*) > 1
GROUP BY author;
    ERROR 1111 (HY000): Invalid use of group function
    Fix: HAVING COUNT(*) > 1 after GROUP BY.
Three mistakes to expect, and how to coach each
  • A plain column next to an aggregate. SELECT title, COUNT(*) (1140) or an extra column with GROUP BY (1055). Coach with the piles: "Point at the pile. Which ONE title belongs in its row?" Then the three fixes: aggregate it, group by it, drop it.
  • The aggregate in WHERE. WHERE COUNT(*) > 1 (1111). Coach with the run order: "When WHERE runs, do the piles exist yet?" Then move it to HAVING.
  • Trusting the group order, or forgetting ORDER BY. The result looks sorted on one run and not on the next server. Coach: "Who promised you that order?" Every grouped query in the project ends with ORDER BY (the project's checklist says so).

Checks for understanding

Ask these out loud during the demo and the lab:

  • COUNT(*) or COUNT(city): which one counts Eve? Answer: COUNT(*): it counts rows; COUNT(city) skips her NULL.
  • Where does price > 10 go, and where does AVG(price) > 10 go? Answer: WHERE (one row's column) and HAVING (a group's aggregate).
  • Why does SELECT author, title, COUNT(*) ... GROUP BY author fail? Answer: Tolkien's pile holds two titles; MySQL will not pick one (1055).
  • What does SUM return when no row matches? Answer: NULL (COUNT returns 0); COALESCE turns it into 0.

3.4

Subqueries & Nested Queries

80 min
Objectives (from the lesson page)
  • Compare every row with an aggregate using a scalar subquery ("above average", "equal to the maximum")
  • Filter with IN, NOT IN, ANY and ALL subqueries, and keep NULLs from emptying a NOT IN
  • Write correlated subqueries and EXISTS / NOT EXISTS checks that compare a row with related rows
  • Build derived tables in FROM and computed columns in SELECT
  • Read and fix the subquery errors 1242, 1241, 1248 and 1235
Timing in the room
10mFrame: fill in the blank; inside out; the three shapes of result (one value, a list, a table)
30mLive demo: scalar, the MAX row, IN, the NOT IN trap, correlated, NOT EXISTS, a derived table, errors 1242 and 1248
25mGuided lab: page_checks_starter.sql, then city_neighbors_starter.sql

Mental model to say

Point at the blank on the board: "WHERE price > ( ___ ). Before the break you would have typed 10.99 there. A subquery is a whole SELECT in that blank: the inner query works out the value, the outer query uses it. Read nested queries from the inside out."

Then the shapes: "What the blank expects depends on where it is. After = or >: ONE value (a scalar). After IN: one column, any number of rows (a list). In FROM: a whole table, and it needs a name. Most subquery errors are a shape mismatch: 1242 is too many rows, 1241 too many columns, 1248 a table without a name."

Then correlation, slowly: "Every subquery so far could run on its own. A correlated one cannot: it mentions the OUTER row. Picture MySQL walking down the books one at a time and asking the inner question again for each one: what is the average of THIS book's author?"

Live demo (you type, they follow)

The lesson's examples, typed live. Run every inner query on its own first (select it and run it), then the whole statement: it is the habit that makes nested queries readable.

  1. Two steps, by hand (2m). "Which books cost more than average? First the average." 
    SELECT AVG(price) FROM books;
    +------------+
| AVG(price) |
+------------+
|  10.990000 |
+------------+
    Type WHERE price > 10.99 once and show it works, then ask: "what happens to that 10.99 next week when a price changes?"
  2. Put the first query where the number was (3m). "Parentheses, a whole SELECT inside. MySQL works out the inner one first, then uses its value." 
    SELECT title, price
FROM books
WHERE price > (SELECT AVG(price) FROM books)
ORDER BY price;
    +-----------------------+-------+
| title                 | price |
+-----------------------+-------+
| To Kill a Mockingbird | 11.99 |
| Dune                  | 14.99 |
| Lord of the Rings     | 15.99 |
+-----------------------+-------+
    Read it inside out, aloud. This is the whole idea of the lesson; everything after is a variation.
  3. The row behind the maximum (2m). "Lesson 3.3 could not say which book costs the most. Now:" 
    SELECT title, author, price
FROM books
WHERE price = (SELECT MAX(price) FROM books);
    +-------------------+----------------+-------+
| title             | author         | price |
+-------------------+----------------+-------+
| Lord of the Rings | J.R.R. Tolkien | 15.99 |
+-------------------+----------------+-------+
    Ask: what would ORDER BY price DESC LIMIT 1 do if two books tied? The subquery returns all of them.
  4. A list: IN with a GROUP BY inside (4m). "One column, several rows: that is a list, and lists go with IN." Run the inner query alone first, highlighted. 
    SELECT first_name, city
FROM customers
WHERE city IN (
    SELECT city FROM customers
    GROUP BY city
    HAVING COUNT(*) > 1
)
ORDER BY city, first_name;
    +------------+----------+
| first_name | city     |
+------------+----------+
| Bob        | Chicago  |
| Grace      | Chicago  |
| Alice      | New York |
| Frank      | New York |
+------------+----------+
    The inner query also returns NULL (Eve and Hank form a group of two). In a plain IN that does no harm.
  5. NOT IN, and the NULL trap (5m). "Which January customers live in a city where no February customer lives? First, the February cities." 
    SELECT city FROM customers WHERE created_at >= '2026-02-01';
    +---------+
| city    |
+---------+
| Chicago |
| NULL    |
+---------+
  6. The trap springs. "Predict how many rows." Then run it. 
    SELECT first_name, city
FROM customers
WHERE created_at < '2026-02-01'
  AND city NOT IN (
      SELECT city FROM customers
      WHERE created_at >= '2026-02-01'
  );
    Empty set
    No error, no rows. city != 'Chicago' AND city != NULL: the second half is unknown for every row. Write the rule: every NOT IN subquery gets an IS NOT NULL.
  7. The fix. Add one line inside the subquery. 
    SELECT first_name, city
FROM customers
WHERE created_at < '2026-02-01'
  AND city NOT IN (
      SELECT city FROM customers
      WHERE created_at >= '2026-02-01'
        AND city IS NOT NULL
  )
ORDER BY first_name;
    +------------+-------------+
| first_name | city        |
+------------+-------------+
| Alice      | New York    |
| Carol      | Los Angeles |
| Dave       | Houston     |
| Frank      | New York    |
+------------+-------------+
    Eve is missing too: her own city is NULL, so NOT IN cannot decide about her. NOT EXISTS treats her differently (Step 9).
  8. Correlated: above their OWN author's average (5m). "Now the inner query depends on the outer row. b is the book being tested, b2 the scan of the same table." Walk the Hobbit row, then the Lord of the Rings row, out loud. 
    SELECT b.title, b.author, b.price
FROM books AS b
WHERE b.price > (
    SELECT AVG(b2.price)
    FROM books AS b2
    WHERE b2.author = b.author
);
    +-------------------+----------------+-------+
| title             | author         | price |
+-------------------+----------------+-------+
| Lord of the Rings | J.R.R. Tolkien | 15.99 |
+-------------------+----------------+-------+
    Every single-book author's average is that book's price, and a price is never above itself: only one row survives.
  9. NOT EXISTS (3m). "Is there ANOTHER customer in my city? NOT EXISTS keeps the ones where there is not." SELECT 1 means: the columns do not matter. 
    SELECT c.first_name, c.city
FROM customers AS c
WHERE NOT EXISTS (
    SELECT 1
    FROM customers AS other
    WHERE other.city = c.city
      AND other.id != c.id
)
ORDER BY c.first_name;
    +------------+-------------+
| first_name | city        |
+------------+-------------+
| Carol      | Los Angeles |
| Dave       | Houston     |
| Eve        | NULL        |
| Hank       | NULL        |
+------------+-------------+
    Eve and Hank appear: nobody can be found in an unknown city. No NULL trap.
  10. A derived table: a summary of a summary (3m). "Count per author first, then average the counts. You cannot write AVG(COUNT(*)) in one level." 
    SELECT ROUND(AVG(book_count), 2) AS avg_books_per_author,
       MAX(book_count)            AS most_books
FROM (
    SELECT author, COUNT(*) AS book_count
    FROM books
    GROUP BY author
) AS author_counts;
    +----------------------+------------+
| avg_books_per_author | most_books |
+----------------------+------------+
|                 1.11 |          2 |
+----------------------+------------+
  11. A whole-table number next to every row (1m). "A subquery in the SELECT list: per-row and whole-table side by side." 
    SELECT
    title,
    price,
    ROUND(price - (SELECT AVG(price) FROM books), 2) AS diff_from_avg
FROM books
ORDER BY diff_from_avg DESC, title
LIMIT 4;
    +-----------------------+-------+---------------+
| title                 | price | diff_from_avg |
+-----------------------+-------+---------------+
| Lord of the Rings     | 15.99 |          5.00 |
| Dune                  | 14.99 |          4.00 |
| To Kill a Mockingbird | 11.99 |          1.00 |
| The Hobbit            | 10.99 |          0.00 |
+-----------------------+-------+---------------+
  12. Error 1242 on purpose (1m). "= needs ONE value. Two books are out of stock." 
    SELECT title, price
FROM books
WHERE price = (SELECT price FROM books WHERE in_stock = FALSE);
    ERROR 1242 (21000): Subquery returns more than 1 row
    Fix: IN, or MIN/MAX. This one works today and breaks the day a second row arrives.
  13. Error 1248 on purpose. "A table in FROM must have a name." 
    SELECT AVG(book_count)
FROM (
    SELECT author, COUNT(*) AS book_count
    FROM books
    GROUP BY author
);
    ERROR 1248 (42000): Every derived table must have its own alias
    Fix: AS author_counts after the closing parenthesis.
Three mistakes to expect, and how to coach each
  • A shape mismatch. = with a list (1242), two columns in an IN list (1241), a derived table without a name (1248). Coach: "Run the inner query alone. How many rows and columns came back? What does the blank expect?"
  • NOT IN with a NULL in the list (silent). An empty result and no error. Coach: "Run the inner query alone. Do you see a NULL?" Then the rule: every NOT IN subquery gets IS NOT NULL, or use NOT EXISTS.
  • A correlated subquery without aliases (silent). WHERE author = author returns the store average. Coach: "Inside the parentheses, which table does author mean?" Then alias both copies and qualify every column inside the subquery.

Checks for understanding

Ask these out loud during the demo and the lab:

  • What shape does WHERE price = ( ... ) expect? And IN ( ... )? Answer: one value; one column with any number of rows.
  • Why did the NOT IN query return nothing? Answer: the list held a NULL, and city != NULL is unknown for every row.
  • What makes a subquery correlated? Answer: it refers to a column of the outer query's current row (b2.author = b.author).
  • Why does FROM (SELECT ...) need AS name? Answer: every table in FROM must have a name (1248).

Closing

Exit ticket (3 questions, no notes, 4 minutes)

Learners answer in the workbook's Exit Ticket box or on a sticky note. Sort the notes before Session 6: question 2's pile tells you whether to reteach NOT IN in the Session 6 warm-up; question 3's pile tells you who to sit with during the 4.1 migration.

  1. Write one query condition that belongs in WHERE and one that belongs in HAVING, for the books table. Answer: for example WHERE in_stock = TRUE (one row's column) and HAVING COUNT(*) > 1 or HAVING AVG(price) > 10 (a group's aggregate).
  2. A NOT IN subquery returns ('Chicago', NULL). What does the outer query return, and what are two fixes? Answer: no rows at all. Add AND city IS NOT NULL inside the subquery, or rewrite with NOT EXISTS.
  3. In your own words: what makes a subquery correlated, and why does it need aliases? Answer: it uses a column of the outer query's current row, so it is re-evaluated per row; with the same table twice, aliases (b, b2) are the only way to say which copy each column belongs to.
